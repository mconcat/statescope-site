How does a hybrid language model count characters to the end of a line?

Gurnee et al. traced how an attention model turns a character count into the distance to a line end. How does Qwen3.5-0.8B do it when most of its mixers are recurrent? With statescope, we release a reader and two dictionaries for every layer of Qwen3.5-0.8B and Qwen3.5-4B, one that represents residual-stream states with groups of four learned directions and one that describes the MLP’s operations. We trace a recurrent head’s character clock as a curve in one learned state group, then follow a layer-12 head that combines count with a width read from earlier lines. Moving just the part of that head’s width edit inside another group’s frame brings the line-break margin to its threshold earlier and shortens the lines the model generates.

Where does the model keep the count?

Fifty characters almost fill a narrow line. A wide line still has room. In Claude 3.5 Haiku, attention heads track the count and combine it with line width. We follow the same task through the GatedDeltaNet layers of Qwen3.5-0.8B. Gurnee et al., When Models Manipulate Manifolds

The first token of each line resets the clock in layer 9, head {{demo.head}}. Its cumulative log decay falls linearly with the number of characters since line start (R² {{lc.clock_r2}} at every width). Group {{demo.l9_group}} holds the count as a curve in its frame, a set of four learned directions. As the state travels along the curve, MLP transforms {{demo.l9_transform_short}} and {{demo.l9_transform_long}} respond to different stretches of it.

We trained the dictionaries without the counting task. To choose the group, we ranked all {{state.groups}} layer-9 groups by the fraction of the squared change in mixer output between paired texts that lay inside each frame. Group {{demo.l9_group}} came first in each condition on both halves of the pairs, with {{pick.min}} to {{pick.max}} of the change, against {{pick.next_min}} to {{pick.next_max}} for the runner-up.

We ranked the early-chain units on odd-numbered pairs, tested the swaps on the {{demo.items_per_half}} even-numbered pairs per condition and tested the selected subspaces again on new documents. Earlier work had identified head {{demo.head}}. In the count conditions we move the last line start and keep the wrapping width fixed. In the width condition we keep the last line start and rewrap the preceding text more widely.

For example, at width 60, swapping that head’s read accounts for {{ci|count:60|head_o}} of the clean-to-corrupt difference in the mean line-break margin. Swapping the whole mixer accounts for {{ci|count:60|layer_y}}. Parentheses give bootstrap intervals over pairs.

The line start resets a clock

The first token of each line starts a new counting signal in the character-clock head (layer 9, head {{demo.head}}). Its cumulative log decay falls by {{lc.clock_low}} to {{lc.clock_high}} per character. The count appears as a curve in state group {{lc.g9}}.

Straight cuts select ranges of count

Each transform reads the count curve along one direction and switches on when that reading crosses its threshold (Figure {{fig|read}}). For the following switching points we group tokens by count and compare the mean gate input with its threshold. The short-line transform ({{demo.l9_transform_short}}) switches on at about {{lc.t410_on_slope}} times the line width and off at {{lc.t410_off_low}} to {{lc.t410_off_high}} characters. The long-line transform ({{demo.l9_transform_long}}) switches on at {{lc.t1749_on_low}} to {{lc.t1749_on_high}} characters. Neither fires a fixed distance before the line end.

Width supplies the missing distance

The model must combine width with count to obtain the distance to the line end. Width already displaces the count curves into a sheet: per character, the displacement across widths is {{lc.sheet_ratio_low}} to {{lc.sheet_ratio_high}} as large as the movement along a line. A memory head (layer 12, head 13) reads width information from earlier lines. Layers 10 and 11 carry the sheet on.

One write brings the line ends together

The same memory head multiplies its width-dependent read by a gate that follows the count, then adds the result to the residual stream. Its share of this mixer’s count curve, using the same within-width projection, is {{lc.h13_curve}}. In the two directions drawn, the means at equal count have a spread of {{pv.s4_count}}, against {{pv.s4_remaining}} at equal remaining characters. After the write, the spread at equal remaining distance is {{pv.s5_remaining}}, against {{pv.s5_count}} at equal count. We infer that the product converts the count code into a code for the remaining characters.

What changes when we edit the width read?

We held the text fixed at width 100 and shifted head 13’s memory read by the mean read at width 40 minus the mean at width 100. On {{lc.cz_tokens}} word-final tokens from the third line onward in {{m|width|documents}} documents, we measured the newline token’s logit minus the logsumexp for space-starting tokens that contain no line break. We smoothed the mean margin over three neighbouring counts and recorded its first upward crossing from count ten onward at {{m|width|level}} logits.

The crossing moved from {{m|width|rows|0|crossing}} characters to {{m|width|rows|1|crossing}} under the head edit, with 95% intervals over documents. Keeping just the change inside group {{lc.g12}}’s frame at resid_mid.12 gave {{m|width|rows|3|crossing}}. The rest gave {{m|width|rows|4|crossing}}, and permuting the head’s channels gave {{m|width|rows|2|crossing}}.

Moving the group’s coordinates directly by the difference between its mean codes at the two widths, matched by count, gives a crossing of {{m|width|rows|5|crossing}}. Swaps and doses confirm the first links

Each item pairs {{demo.tokens_per_text}}-token texts ending on the same words. In every cell, the clean text's last line is full, at most {{demo.clean_short_max}} characters short of the width, so a line break is due. In the corrupt text a line break is not due.

A cell is a task setting defined by the wrapping width and how the corrupt text is made. The three cells are count:60, count:100 and width:100. Each cell contains {{demo.items_per_cell}} pairs.

  • In the count cells, the corrupt text's last line starts at a later word than the clean text's, chosen so that the line holds between {{demo.corrupt_fill_low}}% and {{demo.corrupt_fill_high}}% of the width at the last word, and the preceding lines are wrapped backward from that word at the same width, moving their line breaks too.
  • In the width cell, the corrupt text's last line starts at the same word as the clean text's, and the preceding lines are wrapped backward at {{demo.width_factor}} times the width.

For the L8–L9 demo, the margin is the last-token logsumexp of newline-token logits minus the logsumexp of logits for tokens starting with a space. A higher margin favours a line break.

m = logsumexp(logits of newline tokens) - logsumexp(logits of tokens that start with a space)

A patch replaces a tensor in the clean run with a value built from the corrupt run and moved onto the clean run's token positions. Each row called a swap on this page is one such patched run of the clean texts. A share is the part of the clean/corrupt margin difference carried by the patched tensor. At 0 the mean margin equals the clean mean. At 1 it equals the corrupt mean. Individual items can move in either direction. Shares can be negative or exceed 1, and shares along a path can overlap.

share = mean(m_clean - m_patched) / mean(m_clean - m_corrupt)

Figure {{fig|chain}} ties each step to its swaps. In every step, the swap of one named unit stands next to the swap of the whole tensor it belongs to, measured by the same route.

Routes, pass criteria and shares for all three conditions

We applied the whole mixer swap or head {{demo.head}}’s swap in full, projected inside group {{demo.l9_group}} at resid_mid.9, or with that projection removed. The changed state enters the MLP dictionary. We patch the writes of six transforms ({{demo.six}}), the writes of just {{demo.l9_transform_short}} and {{demo.l9_transform_long}}, or let all downstream paths respond.

Repetition rules travel through a plane

In the repetition task, “sun, tree, sun” follows ABA and “sun, tree, tree” follows ABB. At the third item, a plane in group {{aba.group}} separates whether a word repeats from which word it repeats. Attention head 14 of layer 15 reads both directions and writes at the answer position. How the dictionaries connect a state to the operations that read it

We use a state dictionary to describe the shapes in the residual stream and an MLP dictionary to describe the cuts and moves applied to them; Figure {{fig|layer}} shows where each reads. Qwen3.5-4B has {{b4.layers}} layers of width {{b4.width}}, and Qwen3.5-0.8B has {{model.layers}} layers of width {{model.width}}. The examples in this section come from Qwen3.5-0.8B.

State dictionary: groups

Our state dictionary uses the Grassmannian block-sparse featurizer (BSF) of Fel et al. (2026). We train one dictionary on the residual stream at two sites in each layer. The site resid_mid.L is the input of the norm before the MLP. The site resid_post.L is resid_mid plus the MLP output.

Each group projects the normalized input onto four orthonormal directions, its frame, and scales the coordinates by a learned factor for that group. It writes through the same frame. The dictionary selects the {{state.active}} groups with the largest decoded contribution norms among {{state.groups}} groups and sums their writes. A group can stay selected while its coordinates change with every character.

Group {{demo.l8_group}} at resid_post.8 marks the first token of a line. Group {{demo.l9_group}} at resid_mid.9 holds the character count as a curve.

We can inspect a group's activity and write, or change its coordinates and decode them back into the residual stream.

MLP dictionary: transforms

The MLP dictionary represents the layer's MLP operations, taking the post-norm MLP input and reconstructing the raw MLP output. Our MLP dictionary’s form, a sparse mixture of linear transforms (MOLT), follows Lindsey et al. (2025), with Oldfield et al. (2025) providing closely related work on decomposing MLP layers into sparse mixtures of linear maps.

A transform compares a linear reading of the normalized MLP input to a learned threshold. This is the straight cut. When the gate opens, the transform writes an input-dependent, gated low-rank update.

We can inspect a transform's gate and write, or change its gate and recompute the output.

A related approach, the subspace-aware sparse autoencoder (SASA) of Dalili and Mahdavi, selects groups with a top-s gate and uses a nuclear-norm penalty to regularize their effective rank. In our BSF, four directions per group is a design choice. The same layer dictionary serves both sides of the MLP, with separate normalization at each site.

We read mixers at the level of the model’s own heads so that we can test its recurrent memory directly. A stateful transcoder trained on 18 GatedDeltaNet layers reproduced {{transcoder.mlp}} percent of the counting head’s response to a layer-8 MLP-output swap and {{transcoder.residual}} percent for a whole-residual swap.
