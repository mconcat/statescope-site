Reading language model computation as circuits of state groups and transforms

Dictionary weights are available for Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-0.8B so far; more models will follow.

Abstract

With statescope, we read computation as shapes and operations: a state group holds a shape in a small frame, a transform makes a straight cut and a low-rank move, and a mixer head carries or combines shapes. The reader is one Python file. We provide dictionary sets for Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-0.8B, with more models to follow.

In Qwen3.5-0.8B, we follow how the model decides where to break a line. The character count forms a curve, but the first straight cuts through it fire by count rather than at a fixed distance from the line end. One head multiplies a width-dependent memory read by a gate that follows the count; we infer that this write turns the count into the distance to the line end. A straight cut then selects positions near the line end across widths.

In Qwen3.5-4B, a group drawn at random holds a signed axis from fatal to vital, while a rule plane is carried and averaged by one attention head. Two transforms also cut the same comparison axis in opposite directions.

Frames hold shapes; transforms act on them

We use a state dictionary to describe the shapes in the residual stream and an MLP dictionary to describe the cuts and moves applied to them; Figure {{fig|layer}} shows where each reads. Qwen3.5-4B has {{b4.layers}} layers of width {{b4.width}}, and Qwen3.5-0.8B has {{model.layers}} layers of width {{model.width}}. The examples in this section come from Qwen3.5-0.8B.

State dictionary: groups

The state dictionary represents the residual stream at two sites of its layer. The site resid_mid.L is the input of the norm before the MLP. The site resid_post.L is resid_mid plus the MLP output.

A state group holds a shape in a small frame of {{state.group_size}} directions, with each token represented by its coordinates. The {{state.active}} active groups among {{state.groups}} groups sum their writes to represent the residual stream.

Group {{demo.l8_group}} at resid_post.8 marks the first token of a line. Group {{demo.l9_group}} at resid_mid.9 holds the character count as a curve.

We can inspect a group's activity and write, or change its coordinates and decode them back into the residual stream.

MLP dictionary: transforms

The MLP dictionary represents the layer's MLP operations, taking the post-norm MLP input and reconstructing the raw MLP output. Our MLP dictionary’s form, a sparse mixture of linear transforms (MOLT), follows Lindsey et al. (2025), with Oldfield et al. (2025) providing closely related work on decomposing MLP layers into sparse mixtures of linear maps.

A transform makes a straight cut by reading one direction against a threshold, then writes a low-rank move scaled by its gate.

In Figure {{fig|lead}}, layer-8 transform {{demo.l8_transform}} fires on {{demo.t189_line_first}} of first tokens, {{demo.t189_newline}} of line-break tokens and {{demo.t189_other}} of other tokens. Layer-9 transform {{demo.l9_transform_short}} fires on {{rate|L9 transform 410|40-49}} to {{rate|L9 transform 410|30-39}} of tokens at counts of 20 to 49 characters, falling to {{rate|L9 transform 410|70-79}} at 70 to 79 characters. Transform {{demo.l9_transform_long}} fires on {{rate|L9 transform 1749|60-69}} of tokens below 70 characters and {{rate|L9 transform 1749|90-99}} at 90 to 99 characters.

We can inspect a transform's gate and write, or change its gate and recompute the output.

By design, the mixers are read directly at the level of the model's own heads and channels. We also trained a stateful transcoder, a dictionary with a state carried across tokens, on 18 GatedDeltaNet layers of the 0.8B model: in the line-counting computation it reproduced about one third of the true head's response, while one of the model's own heads (layer 9, head {{demo.head}}) carried the count about as well as the whole mixer. Attention is read at the level of heads.

From characters counted to a line break

The line-counting task studied by Gurnee et al. asks how a model decides where to break text wrapped to a fixed width. We follow the same task in Qwen3.5-0.8B. To predict a line break at the last word, the model needs the characters so far and the width.

The line start resets a clock

The first token resets head {{demo.head}} of the layer-9 mixer with write strength {{lc.beta_first}}. This head is a clock: the logarithm of its decay falls by {{lc.clock_low}} to {{lc.clock_high}} per character, with R² {{lc.clock_r2}} at every width. It carries {{lc.h10_curve}} of the mixer's count curve, which appears in group {{lc.g9}}'s frame. By design, we read mixers through the model's own heads and MLPs through transforms.

Straight cuts select ranges of count

A transform cuts the shape straight, using a read direction and threshold as shown in Figure {{fig|read}}. Transform {{demo.l9_transform_short}} switches on at about {{lc.t410_on_slope}} times the width and off at {{lc.t410_off_low}} to {{lc.t410_off_high}} characters. Transform {{demo.l9_transform_long}} switches on at {{lc.t1749_on_low}} to {{lc.t1749_on_high}} characters. Neither fires a fixed distance before the line end.

Width supplies the missing distance

The model must combine width with count to obtain the distance to the line end. Width already displaces the count curves into a sheet: per character, the displacement across widths is {{lc.sheet_ratio_low}} to {{lc.sheet_ratio_high}} as large as the movement along a line. Head 13 of the layer-12 mixer keeps width information in a memory read from earlier lines. Layers 10 and 11 carry the sheet on.

One write brings the line ends together

Head 13 of the layer-12 mixer multiplies the width it reads from memory by a gate that follows the count, then adds the result; it carries {{lc.h13_curve}} of the mixer's count curve. Before the mixer's write, tokens with equal count sit close together across widths: their spread is {{pv.s4_count}}, against {{pv.s4_remaining}} at equal remaining characters. After the write, tokens with equal remaining characters sit close together: their spread is {{pv.s5_remaining}}, against {{pv.s5_count}} at equal count. We infer that the product converts the count code into a code for the remaining characters.

A straight cut now marks the line end

Transform {{lc.t2490}} of the layer-13 MLP fires {{lc.t2490_rem_low}} to {{lc.t2490_rem_high}} characters before the end across all {{lc.n_widths}} widths, and its cut is the straight line drawn at resid_mid.13 in Figure {{fig|path}}, within {{pv.t2490_error}} characters of its measured switching points. Before the write, at resid_mid.9, no cut fires a fixed distance before the line end. Layer-12 transform {{lc.t2069}} fires {{lc.t2069_rem_low}} to {{lc.t2069_rem_high}} characters before the end, and layer-20 transform 130 fires at {{lc.t130_rem_low}} to {{lc.t130_rem_high}} remaining characters. Head 15 of the layer-21 mixer writes the line-break decision, with {{lc.h15_remaining}} of its write explained by remaining characters. Its direct shares of the decision difference are {{lc.h15_direct.count60}}, {{lc.h15_direct.count100}} and {{lc.h15_direct.width100}} in count:60, count:100 and width:100, respectively.

Figure {{fig|read}} shows how the layer-9 transforms {{demo.l9_transform_short}} and {{demo.l9_transform_long}} read the count curve along different directions and compare each reading with a threshold.

Swapping the pieces moves the decision

Swapping pieces between paired texts moves the model's line-break preference. In Figure {{fig|causal}}, we shift head 13's memory read in texts wrapped at 100 characters by the mean difference between widths 40 and 100, across {{lc.cz_tokens}} word-final tokens.

At count {{lc.cz_bump_count}}, the mean preference for a line break over a space reaches {{lc.cz_bump_margin}} logits, compared with {{lc.cz_clean_there}} unchanged, {{lc.cz_perm_there}} with permuted channels and {{lc.cz_end_clean}} at a clean line end. The first count where this margin exceeds -3 moves from {{lc.cz_cstar_clean}} to {{lc.cz_cstar_shift40}}, while the permuted-channel control gives {{lc.cz_cstar_perm}}.

Swaps and doses confirm the first links

Each item pairs {{demo.tokens_per_text}}-token texts ending on the same words. In every cell, the clean text's last line is full, at most {{demo.clean_short_max}} characters short of the width, so a line break is due. In the corrupt text a line break is not due.

A cell is a task setting defined by the wrapping width and how the corrupt text is made. The three cells are count:60, count:100 and width:100. Each cell contains {{demo.items_per_cell}} pairs.

  • In the count cells, the corrupt text's last line starts at a later word than the clean text's, chosen so that the line holds between {{demo.corrupt_fill_low}}% and {{demo.corrupt_fill_high}}% of the width at the last word, and the preceding lines are wrapped backward from that word at the same width, moving their line breaks too.
  • In the width cell, the corrupt text's last line starts at the same word as the clean text's, and the preceding lines are wrapped backward at {{demo.width_factor}} times the width.

The margin is the last-token logsumexp of newline-token logits minus the logsumexp of logits for tokens starting with a space. A higher margin favours a line break.

m = logsumexp(logits of newline tokens) - logsumexp(logits of tokens that start with a space)

A patch replaces a tensor in the clean run with a value built from the corrupt run and moved onto the clean run's token positions. Each row called a swap on this page is one such patched run of the clean texts. A share is the part of the clean/corrupt margin difference carried by the patched tensor. A share of 0 means that the patch changes nothing. A share of 1 means that the patch alone turns the clean run into the corrupt one.

share = mean(m_clean - m_patched) / mean(m_clean - m_corrupt)

Figure {{fig|chain}} ties each step to its swaps. In every step, the swap of one named unit stands next to the swap of the whole tensor it belongs to, measured by the same route. We read shares on the {{demo.items_per_half}} even-numbered items of each cell and chose the units on the odd-numbered items.

In the count cells, transform {{demo.l8_transform}} or group {{demo.l8_group}} carries about as much as the whole L8 MLP output through the L9 mixer. Head {{demo.head}}'s read alone gives a larger share than the whole mixer output. Transform {{demo.l9_transform_short}} carries the L9 MLP's effect in count:60, and transform {{demo.l9_transform_long}} carries it in width:100. The same computation is also visible in individual MLP neurons.

From the L9 mixer to the L9 transforms

The last link asks how the mixer's write reaches the L9 transforms. The whole mixer swap and head {{demo.head}}'s swap are each applied in full, only inside the {{demo.frame_dim}}-dimensional frame of state group {{demo.l9_group}} of resid_mid.9, or as the rest. The share is then read by three routes. For the first two, the changed resid_mid.9 enters the L9 MLP dictionary, and only the change in the selected transforms' writes is patched at the MLP output: once for the six L9 transforms we selected ({{demo.six}}), and once for transforms {{demo.l9_transform_short}} and {{demo.l9_transform_long}} alone. The third route is the same change with everything downstream.

We registered a pass rule for the mixer-to-MLP link. The rule requires at least {{rule.inside_min}} of the whole share inside the frame, at most {{rule.rest_max_fraction}} of it and at most {{rule.rest_max}} in the rest, and at most {{rule.random_max}} in random frames, in both count cells. Our verdict was that the rule holds for the route through the six transforms and holds only in part for the route through {{demo.l9_transform_short}} and {{demo.l9_transform_long}} alone, because we recorded a share of {{rule.recorded_rest}} for the rest in count:100 through those two transforms.

Steering one unit by a dose

A dose α scales the change from a unit’s clean value toward its corrupt value, aligned to the clean text’s token positions, and we varied the dose across {{steer.doses}} values from {{steer.dose_min}} to {{steer.dose_max}} for {{steer.units}} units. The shares in Figure {{fig|steer}} are over all {{steer.items}} items of a cell, while we read shares elsewhere on the page on the {{demo.items_per_half}} even-numbered items, so the numbers differ. For L8 transform {{demo.l8_transform}} in count:60, over all {{steer.items}} items, we observed a share of {{steer|count:60|l8/t189|1}} at the full swap and {{steer|count:60|l8/t189|2}} at α = {{steer.dose_max}}, so going past the full swap adds nothing there, while the share of {{steer|count:60|l8/t189|-1}} at α = {{steer.dose_min}} shows that a negative dose is not the mirror image of a positive one.

The same operations in a larger model

In Qwen3.5-4B, we find the same operations: frames hold shapes, heads carry and combine them, and transforms cut and move them. The set covers {{b4.layers}} layers of width {{b4.width}}, with {{b4.groups}} groups of {{b4.group_size}} directions per state dictionary and {{b4.transforms}} transforms per MLP dictionary.

A frame holds signed distinctions

We drew groups at random and traced them by hand, including group {{leth.group}} at resid_post.{{leth.layer}}. Among {{leth.words}} adjectives completing “It was a W”, its fourth coordinate orders lethality: fatal {{leth.fatal}}, safe {{leth.safe}} and vital {{leth.vital}}. Its second coordinate is negative for vital {{leth.c2_vital}}, fatal {{leth.c2_fatal}} and pivotal {{leth.c2_pivotal}}; our reading is that these words describe something decisive.

From the same draw, group {{tab.group}} at resid_post.{{tab.layer}} distinguishes table separator cells followed by another column, at {{tab.more_low}} to {{tab.more_high}}, from the last column, at {{tab.last_low}} to {{tab.last_high}}. Group {{ex.group}} at resid_mid.{{ex.layer}} places expressions introducing examples in one corner and the listed examples in another.

A head carries a plane

We follow the repetition task from Yang et al. (2025): ABA gives “sun, tree, sun” and ABB gives “sun, tree, tree”; a related later study is Li and Webb. At the third item, group {{aba.group}} at resid_post.{{aba.site_layer}} holds a repeat axis with values {{aba.aba_common}} for ABA and {{aba.abb_common}} for ABB, against {{aba.ctrl_common}} for new words, and a which-item axis with values {{aba.aba_rule}} and {{aba.abb_rule}}.

Selection alone marks repetition: group {{aba.g13}} at resid_post.13 is selected on {{aba.sel_aba}} of ABA items, {{aba.sel_abb}} of ABB items and {{aba.sel_ctrl}} of new-word items.

Attention head 14 of layer 15 reads each axis along its own direction and writes it at the answer position, with gains {{aba.read1_gain}} and {{aba.read2_gain}}, against random directions with median {{aba.random_gain}} and maximum {{aba.random_gain_max}}. Moving between rules inside this frame, only in what the mixer reads, moves the answer state by {{aba.move_low}} to {{aba.move_high}} of the natural difference, compared with {{aba.move_random_low}} to {{aba.move_random_high}} for an equally sized move in a random frame.

The same head averages across lines

With {{avg.lines}} mixed example lines in all {{avg.histories}} orders, the same head forms a running average at the answer position. The states lie near a plane ordered by type counts, which explain {{avg.count_distance}} of the variance in distances between histories, compared with {{avg.shared_suffix}} for shared ending length.

As lines accumulate, measured shrink factors are {{avg.shrink2}}, {{avg.shrink3}}, {{avg.shrink4}} and {{avg.shrink5}} for two through five lines, against {{avg.ideal2}}, {{avg.ideal3}}, {{avg.ideal4}} and {{avg.ideal5}} for an exact running average. Head 14 gives {{avg.attention_third}} of its attention to third items and writes an attention-weighted sum of vectors for the line types, with R² {{avg.mixture_r2}} against {{avg.equal_r2}} for equal weights.

The plane's coordinates predict the answer's logit difference between the rules with R² {{avg.answer_r2}}.

Two cuts read one axis both ways

We compare digits occupying the same slot on successive lines, as in lists of dates or weights. At resid_mid.7, group {{cmp.group}} already separates less and greater along one axis, with equal values apart on another; the plane holds {{cmp.plane_share}} of the variance of {{cmp.cells}} cell means. Transforms {{b4u.js_transform}} and 472 read less and greater in opposite directions, with cosine {{cmp.cosine}} between their read directions.

Each transform has one cut, while its write rank carries variation with context. Transform {{b4u.js_transform}} has rank {{cmp.rank_357}}, and a fixed write direction would retain {{cmp.fixed_357_list}} of its write on lists and {{cmp.fixed_357_js}} on JavaScript comparisons, compared with {{cmp.fixed_472_list}} and {{cmp.fixed_472_js}} for transform 472. The remaining write varies with the surrounding text, operator and distance between digits.

Moving a state inside a frame changes predictions

We drew group {{leth.group}} at resid_post.{{leth.layer}} at random from the dictionary and traced it by hand: its fourth coordinate orders adjectives by lethality, with “fatal” high and “vital” low. In sentences of the form “It was a W ...”, we move the state of “vital” along that coordinate to a death word’s value inside the frame. The predicted nouns shift towards “mistake” and “accident”: the margin changes by {{leth.move}} nats in {{leth.move_toward}} of {{leth.pairs}} pairs and covers {{leth.move_share}} of the gap; equally sized random moves change it by {{leth.other_low}} to {{leth.other_high}} nats.

We took the difference between prompts with and without an instruction to validate a function’s arguments, measured on the instruction’s tokens at resid_post.{{su.layer}}, split it into groups with the dictionary, and used the largest group alone, group {{su.group}}, to move the state on each of a neutral sentence’s {{su.tokens}} tokens along one direction in a four-dimensional frame. At the instruction’s own mean level, the share of generated functions containing a raise statement or an isinstance check rises from {{su.rate0}} to {{su.rate1}}, exceeding pooled controls at the same size by {{su.minus1}}. At the level of the instruction’s highest tokens, the share reaches {{su.rate2}}, exceeding pooled controls by {{su.minus2}}; the real instruction gives {{su.instructed}}. We infer that this does not simply promote a token: the moved tokens end {{su.gap}} tokens before the prompt ends, the first check appears a median of {{su.first_low}} to {{su.first_high}} tokens into the answer, and giving the unchanged prompt the first {{su.forced_tokens}} tokens of the answer produced after the move leaves the rate at {{su.forced_rate}}. Removing this group from the real instruction’s tokens leaves the rate at {{su.removed}}, while the eight largest groups together give {{su.eight_rate}} at their natural size.

Using it

Python 3.12 or newer is required. This command adds transformers, accelerate and flash-linear-attention, which capture and the demonstration need:

sh
pip install -e '.[model]'

This command downloads one layer of the 4B set:

sh
{{b4.download_one_layer}}

The reader is the single file src/statescope/reader.py. It can be copied next to the weights. capture returns resid_mid, mlp_out and resid_post for the requested layer with position 0 dropped.

python
{{reader.example_4b}}

The 4B model in bf16 plus one layer's two dictionaries needed more than {{b4.gpu_gib}} GiB of GPU memory. The 0.8B set is read the same way with Qwen/Qwen3.5-0.8B and its own folder, with {{mlp.transforms}} transforms per layer and layers {{model.first_layer}} to {{model.last_layer}}.

This command downloads the 0.8B weights:

sh
hf download mconcat/statescope-qwen3.5-0.8b --local-dir <folder>

The first command checks all {{set.files}} files against the manifest. The second re-runs the traced line-count chain; it ran in under two minutes on one RTX 5090, including model loading.

sh
python demo/verify_release.py --release <folder>
python demo/line_count.py --release <folder> --device cuda:0 --out <folder for the result>

Without flash-linear-attention, transformers silently uses a slow GatedDeltaNet reference implementation. The repository contains the reader, a demonstration and tests. Training code will follow.
